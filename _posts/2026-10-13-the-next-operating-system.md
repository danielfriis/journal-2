---
title: The next operating system
description: Most homes will have an AI workstation without a screen. Every other device becomes a thin client.
date: 2026-10-13
---

I think most homes will have an AI workstation. A box in a cupboard, without a screen, running a new kind of operating system. Phones, laptops, and whatever we end up wearing on our faces will be thin clients that connect to it.

I already live a version of this. A Mac Studio in my home runs nothing but an AI agent. Nobody looks at its screen. I reach it from my laptop and get the results back as text, files, or pages.

Today’s operating systems exist to run apps that someone else built and you installed. I don’t think that survives.

I’ve [argued before]({% post_url 2026-10-13-the-value-of-software-is-racing-to-zero %}) that the value of software is racing to zero. You no longer ask for a tool. You ask for a job to be done, and the machine writes whatever software the job needs along the way.

If that’s true, you stop installing apps. The software for a job gets built when the job starts and thrown away when it’s done.

So the new operating system doesn’t manage windows and files. It manages your context: your documents, your history, your preferences, and the credentials to act on your behalf. The interface is a conversation, and the screen is whichever one you happen to be holding.

Why at home and not in the cloud? Your context is the most private data you have, and I’d rather it lived on hardware I own. An agent that works all day is cheaper on a box you’ve paid for than on compute you rent. And it never logs off.

There is a tension here. In the same note, I argued that compute is one of the few things that keeps its value. If compute is scarce, the biggest models will stay in the data centres that can afford them. I don’t think that breaks the idea. The box at home doesn’t need to run the smartest model. It needs to hold your context and decide what to send where: small models locally, hard problems to the cloud.

It looks a lot like the mainframe era, when terminals connected to one central computer. This time, the central computer is yours.
